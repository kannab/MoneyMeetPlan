The 15-minute checkup is a first look, not the plan. Understanding your own money takes real time and attention, and learning how it works is part of the job, not a detour. It's one of the few investments that keeps paying for the rest of your life.

Your FI number, built properly

The popular shortcut is 25 × your annual spending, from the "4% rule": withdraw 4% of your portfolio in the first year, then raise that dollar amount with inflation each year, and a diversified portfolio has historically lasted about 30 years. Treat 25× as a starting point. Four adjustments make it yours:

  1. Start from spending at FI, not today's.Add health insurance you'll pay yourself before Medicare at 65, taxes on withdrawals from pre-tax accounts, and irregular costs like cars and roofs. Remove costs that end, like a mortgage or childcare. "Lean FI" covers essentials only.
  2. Subtract income you can count on.Social Security and any pension shrink what the portfolio must cover. Your Social Security statement at ssa.gov/myaccount shows estimates at 62, full retirement age, and 70, but those assume you keep working until you claim.
  3. Match the multiple to how long retirement lasts.About 25× for a 30-year retirement. For 40 years or more, plan on roughly 29–31× (a 3.25–3.5% withdrawal rate).
  4. Stress-test it.Your AI runs a bad first year in the market, high inflation, a job loss, and a Social Security cut. The retirement trust fund is projected to run short in late 2032, after which about 78% of scheduled benefits would be payable unless Congress acts.

Example. A household spends $60,000 a year and expects $36,000 a year from Social Security at 67. The portfolio covers the other $24,000 plus taxes, about $26,500, so their number for that phase is roughly 25 × $26,500 ≈ $660,000, not $1.5 million. Stopping work before benefits start needs a bridge for those years on top.

Foundations first

Before optimizing anything, check the foundations. Session 1 scores each one, and every gap goes to the top of your plan.

  • An emergency fund of 3–6 months of essential spending in a high-yield savings account (6–12 months with one income, variable pay, or a volatile industry).
  • No debt above about 8% interest.
  • The full employer match, every paycheck.
  • Health insurance that fits your family, and disability coverage for each earner.
  • Term life insurance if anyone depends on your income, including a stay-at-home parent.
  • A will naming a guardian for minor children; current beneficiaries on every account; powers of attorney and healthcare directives.
  • Identity protection: credit frozen at all three bureaus (kids too), an IRS Identity Protection PIN, your own my Social Security account.
  • Savings that happen automatically.

    Other benefits worth knowing

    Not buckets to fill in order, but each can be worth hundreds or thousands of dollars a year, and most are chosen at open enrollment.

    Health FSA

    Pays copays, prescriptions, glasses, and dental work with pre-tax pay: up to $3,400 in 2026. The whole year's amount is available on the first day of the plan year, before you've paid it in. It's use-it-or-lose-it, though a plan may let up to $680 carry over or give you 2½ extra months to spend it. Alongside an HSA, only a limited-purpose (dental and vision) or post-deductible FSA is allowed.

    Worth: running $2,000 of predictable costs through it saves about $590 in the 22% bracket.

    Dependent care FSA

    Pre-tax pay for the care of children under 13, or a dependent adult, so you (and your spouse, if married) can work: daycare, preschool, before- and after-school care, and summer day camp, but not overnight camp. The law now allows up to $7,500 per household, up from $5,000, if your employer's plan adopted the higher limit. Unlike a health FSA, it pays out only what you've put in so far. Every FSA dollar also comes off the child and dependent care credit's cost limit, so a full FSA usually leaves no credit. With one child the FSA usually saves more; with two or more in care and a middle income (roughly $70,000–$130,000 for a couple), the credit alone can save more, so compare: in 2026 the credit is 20–50% of up to $3,000 of costs for one child or $6,000 for two or more, and it can only reduce tax you owe.

    Worth: $7,500 in the 22% bracket saves about $2,200, counting Social Security and Medicare tax.

    Stock purchase plan (ESPP)

    Buy company stock through payroll at up to a 15% discount. The law caps your purchase rights at $25,000 of stock a year, valued at the price on the grant date (usually when the offering starts), and your plan may set a lower cap; ask the administrator how much room you have. A 15% discount is about a 17.6% gain on the day you buy, and a lookback, which uses the lower of two prices, can add more. Selling soon after each purchase locks in the discount and keeps your bet on one company small; your paycheck already rides on it. At tax time, check the cost basis on your 1099-B: it's often too low for ESPP and RSU shares, which taxes the same income twice.

    Worth: $10,000 a year at a 15% discount, sold right away, is about $1,760 before tax.

    RSUs and stock options

    RSUs are taxed as pay when they vest. Withholding is usually a flat 22% (37% above $1 million), which falls short in the 24% bracket and above, and by a lot at 32% or higher, so set the difference aside. Keeping vested shares is the same bet as using a cash bonus to buy company stock: would you? Unvested grants usually vanish when you leave, so know your vesting dates before you change jobs. Stock options have their own tax traps; map them with your AI and a CPA before you exercise.

    Worth knowing: $50,000 vesting in the 32% bracket means $11,000 withheld and about $16,000 owed, so roughly $5,000 more is due at tax time.

    Life and disability at work

    Group life is often 1–2 times salary, and it usually ends when you leave the job; coverage above $50,000 adds a little taxable income to your W-2. Long-term disability often replaces about 60% of base pay, and if your employer pays the premium, the benefit is taxable. Extra coverage through work is convenient but rarely goes with you, so compare it with your own term life and disability policies.

    Worth knowing: your ability to earn is usually your biggest asset. $80,000 a year for 30 years is $2.4 million.

    Smaller perks that add up

    Student loan help or tuition: up to $5,250 a year, tax-free. Commuter transit and parking: up to $340 a month each, pre-tax. An employee assistance program (EAP): free, confidential counseling, and often legal and financial consultations. Also look for backup childcare, a legal plan, wellness money, and discounts you've never opened.

    Worth: $340 a month of transit through payroll saves about $1,200 a year in the 22% bracket.

        Two habits that make this work

        Write your rules before the market tests them

        An Investment Policy Statement (IPS) is two pages that say what you'll own, how much, and what you'll do when things move. Decisions made calmly, in advance, beat decisions made during a headline.

        Decide once, then execute

        Settled decisions go on a "locked decisions" list, and your AI holds you to it. Changing one takes a written reason, a week's wait, and a fresh-eyes review, never a nervous Tuesday.

        Concentration is a household problem. If your salary, bonus, unvested grants, vested shares, and held stock all come from one employer, that's one risk, not five: a bad year can hit your job and your savings at once. The setup measures each part and stress-tests them together.

        Glossary: the terms your AI will use, in plain English
        4% rule
        Withdraw 4% of your portfolio in the first year of retirement, then raise that dollar amount with inflation. Historically it lasted about 30 years.
        529 plan
        A college savings account: growth is tax-free when it pays for education, and many states give a tax break for contributions. Leftovers can partly move to the child's Roth IRA.
        AGI (adjusted gross income)
        Your income after certain deductions, like pre-tax 401(k) contributions. Many tax rules key off it.
        Annuity
        A contract, usually from an insurer, that turns a lump sum into payments. Simple ones can be useful; complex ones often carry high fees and surrender charges.
        Backdoor Roth
        Contributing to a traditional IRA and converting it to a Roth when your income is too high to contribute to a Roth directly.
        Beneficiary designation
        The form naming who inherits an account. It overrides your will.
        Catch-up contribution
        Extra room to save from age 50: in 2026, $8,000 more in a 401(k) ($11,250 more at ages 60–63, if the plan offers it) and $1,100 more in an IRA.
        Cost basis
        What you paid for an investment. Gains above it are taxed when you sell.
        Credit freeze
        A free lock on your credit report that makes it much harder for anyone to open new credit in your name. You lift it for a day when you apply for credit.
        Dependent care FSA
        A workplace account that pays for childcare (kids under 13) with pre-tax pay: up to $7,500 per household in 2026, if your employer's plan allows it.
        Donor-advised fund (DAF)
        An account for charitable giving: you take the deduction when you put money in and grant it to charities later.
        Emergency fund
        Cash for surprises, sized in months of essential spending and kept in a high-yield savings account.
        Employer match
        Money your employer adds to your 401(k) when you contribute. Not taking it leaves pay on the table.
        Expense ratio
        A fund's annual cost as a percentage of what you invest. Broad index funds often charge under 0.1%.
        Fee-only
        An advisor paid only by you, never by commissions. Not the same as "fee-based".
        Fiduciary
        Someone bound to act in your best interest.
        Fraud alert
        A free note on your credit report telling lenders to confirm it's really you. It lasts a year, or seven with an identity theft report.
        Futures
        Contracts to buy or sell something at a set price on a future date, traded with a small deposit and big leverage.
        HDHP (high-deductible health plan)
        A health plan with a higher deductible and usually lower premiums. An HSA-eligible HDHP is what lets you open and fund an HSA.
        HSA (health savings account)
        Available with a high-deductible health plan. Tax-free going in, growing, and coming out for medical costs.
        Index fund
        A fund that owns a whole market, such as every large US company, at low cost.
        IP PIN (Identity Protection PIN)
        A six-digit number from the IRS, new each year, that must be on your tax return so no one else can file as you.
        IPS (Investment Policy Statement)
        Your written rules for what you own, how much, and what you do when things change.
        IRMAA
        Extra Medicare premiums for higher-income retirees, based on your income from two years earlier.
        JTWROS
        Joint tenants with right of survivorship: when one owner dies, the other owns the account automatically.
        Leveraged ETF
        A fund that aims for two or three times a single day's market move. It resets daily, so it isn't built for holding.
        Margin
        Money borrowed from your broker against your investments. It magnifies gains and losses, and you can lose more than you put in.
        Mega backdoor Roth
        Putting after-tax money into a 401(k) above the regular limit, then converting it to Roth. Only some plans allow it.
        Open enrollment
        The yearly window, usually in the fall, to change your workplace health plan, FSAs, and insurance. Outside it, changes need a life event, like a birth or a marriage.
        Option (call or put)
        A contract giving the right to buy (call) or sell (put) 100 shares at a set price before a set date.
        Pro-rata rule
        When you convert IRA money to a Roth, the IRS counts all your pre-tax IRA balances, which can make a backdoor Roth partly taxable.
        Real return
        Investment return after inflation.
        RMD (required minimum distribution)
        Withdrawals the IRS requires from pre-tax retirement accounts, starting in your 70s.
        Roth vs traditional
        Roth accounts are taxed now and tax-free later; traditional accounts are deductible now and taxed later.
        RSU and ESPP
        Restricted stock units (company shares you receive as they vest, taxed as income) and employee stock purchase plans (company shares you buy at a discount).
        Rule of 55
        Leave your job in or after the year you turn 55 and you can withdraw from that employer's 401(k) without the 10% early-withdrawal penalty. Rolling it into an IRA gives this up.
        Saver’s Credit
        A tax credit of 10%, 20%, or 50% of up to $2,000 each person saves for retirement, for households with lower incomes. For 2027 savings it becomes the Saver’s Match, paid into your retirement account.
        Sequence risk
        The danger of a market drop early in retirement, when withdrawals lock in the losses.
        SIM swap
        When a thief gets your phone number moved to their own phone so they receive your texted sign-in codes.
        Target-date fund
        One fund holding a diversified mix that grows more conservative as its target year approaches.
        Tax-loss harvesting
        Selling an investment at a loss to offset taxable gains. If you or your spouse buy the same or a substantially identical investment within 30 days before or after, in any account, the wash-sale rule applies: the loss is postponed, added to the new shares' cost, and if the purchase was in an IRA, it's lost for good.
        TOD and POD
        Transfer on death and payable on death: designations that pass an account straight to a named person without probate.
        True-up
        A year-end top-up of your employer match, for plans that match each paycheck, in case you hit the limit early in the year.
        Trump Account
        A new account for children under 18: up to $5,000 a year from July 4, 2026, invested in US stock index funds and locked until the year the child turns 18, then treated like a traditional IRA. US-citizen children born 2025 through 2028 can get a $1,000 federal deposit.
        Umbrella insurance
        Extra liability coverage on top of your home and auto policies.
        Vesting
        When your employer's contributions become yours to keep. Your own contributions are always 100% yours.

        Your AI, held to a fiduciary standard

        A fiduciary is someone bound to act in your best interest, like a trustee, and unlike a salesperson. With a human advisor, don't assume it from a title or from how they're paid: "fee-only" describes pay, not a legal duty, so ask them to confirm in writing that they act as a fiduciary at all times. An AI can't legally be your fiduciary, but you can hold it to that standard. Your instructions ask it to prefer the simple, low-cost option, tell you when you're wrong, refuse to help you talk yourself into a bad idea, and hold the line on the day you're tempted, and the first test in Step 10 is a quick spot check that it does. As Bhanu puts it: “If I could keep only one instruction, this would be it.”

        What "act as a fiduciary" means in practice

        • Care. It does the work: verifies, runs the numbers in code, shows its assumptions, and says what it doesn't know.
        • Loyalty. Your family's interest comes first, ahead of what you want to hear and ahead of any product, platform, or trend. It tells you how every provider gets paid.
        • Candor. Disagreement stated plainly. Correction over flattery.
        • Under pressure. If you push without a new fact, it restates its position once and asks what would change its mind. Your written rules can't be overridden in the moment, only through a deliberate amendment.
        • Limits. It tells you when a question needs a licensed professional, which one, and exactly what to ask.

        When to bring in a human, and how to check them

        Your AI is the analyst. Some questions need a licensed person: tax (a CPA or enrolled agent), legal and estate documents (an attorney licensed in your state), and a second opinion on the whole plan (a fee-only planner).

        How they're paid

        • Fee-only: paid only by you, hourly, by flat fee, or as a percentage of assets. The cleanest incentives.
        • Fee-based: fees plus commissions on products. Not the same thing, despite the name.
        • Commission: paid by the product company. Common for insurance and annuities.
        • For most households, an hourly or flat-fee, advice-only planner is the best value. Find one through NAPFA, the XY Planning Network, or the Garrett Planning Network.

        How to check them

        • Ask in writing: "Are you a fiduciary at all times, for all of my accounts?"
        • Look them up on the SEC's Investment Adviser Public Disclosure site (adviserinfo.sec.gov), FINRA BrokerCheck (brokercheck.finra.org), and the CFP Board's verify tool (cfp.net).
        • Read their Form CRS and Form ADV Part 2A: how they're paid, their conflicts, and any disciplinary history.
        • Keep your money at an independent custodian in your own name.
        • Size your insurance needs with your AI before meeting an agent, then compare quotes.

        Red flags

        Cash-value life insurance (whole life, indexed universal life) sold as an investment, a "private bank", or a tax shelter.
        Annuities inside an IRA, or with long surrender charges.
        Non-traded REITs, private funds, and anything you can't easily sell.
        Sales loads, wrap fees, or total fees above about 0.5% a year without a written comparison.
        Anyone recommending you roll your 401(k) into their own product.
        "Guaranteed" high returns, pressure to act today, or requests for secrecy.
        High yield sold as safe income. Judge investments on after-tax total return; a high yield is a risk signal.

        Already own one of these? Don't cancel or sell in a hurry. Surrender charges, taxes, and your current health all matter. Have your AI lay out the options, then confirm with a fee-only planner.

        Rules of the road

        Five practices turn a clever chatbot into a money analyst you can check: a written policy, locked decisions, a real amendment process, a market check instead of a panic, and fresh-eyes pressure tests.

        1. The IPS is your constitution

        An Investment Policy Statement is two pages that say what you own and why, what you'll do when things move, and what counts as changing your mind. It holds the rules; the snapshot and action plan hold the numbers. Session 4 drafts it, a fresh-eyes review tests it, and then you do the unglamorous part: date it, both initial it, save it as 02-IPS-v1.0, and add it to the project.

        What a household IPS covers

        • Purpose and goals, with the FI target and the education timeline side by side.
        • A risk assessment first: capacity, tolerance, and the return you actually need.
        • Target allocation for the household with rebalancing bands, then which assets go in which accounts.
        • The contribution order, with income as item zero.
        • A concentration cap for any single stock or employer, with the remedy when it's breached.
        • A play-money cap for speculation, or none.
        • Rules for positions you're reducing: dividend reinvestment off; sell, hold, or donate rules for low-basis shares.
        • The HSA investment rule, the education vehicle and glidepath, and the emergency-fund floor.
        • The big-decision threshold and 48-hour wait, and the market-check rule.
        • The amendment process and an annual review date in October.

        2. Locked decisions

        Every settled decision goes on 03-Locked-Decisions as one line: the rule, the reason, what would justify reopening it, and the date. Your AI holds you to the list even when you push. At each annual review you reconfirm, revise, or retire every lock, so none quietly outlives the facts behind it; until then, each stays in force. Some examples of the kind of thing that gets locked; yours will differ, and each deserves a check with your CPA or attorney:

        Locked rule (example)Why
        We never carry a credit card balance.Card interest beats any investment return.
        The emergency fund stays at [6] months of essentials and is never invested.It's insurance, not an investment.
        401(k) contributions rise one point every January until we reach [target].Automation beats willpower.
        Retirement savings come before college savings.Kids can borrow for college; nobody lends for retirement.
        Retirement accounts: spouse primary, then a trust or custodian for the kids, with an estate attorney choosing the trust language.A minor can inherit but can't manage the money, so an adult must; employer plans also require the spouse as primary unless the spouse waives it in writing.

        1. Write the proposed change and the reason in 06-Decision-Log.

        • Whether the tax rules and limits cited are current.
        • Completeness: Social Security and pensions, taxes, inflation, fees, and health costs before 65.
        • Stress tests: an early market drop, years of high inflation, a lost income, a Social Security cut.
        • Conflicts with your IPS and locked decisions, missing risks, and better alternatives.

        Only with the documents

        • Whether the inputs themselves are right: a mistyped balance, a missed account, a wrong cost basis.
        • So every review lists the inputs it couldn't verify and the 3–5 that would change its verdict.
        • For big decisions, attach the redacted documents for those numbers and run an input audit (prompt H).
        • Then spot-check the same numbers yourself against your statements. You hold the originals.

        A review is a second pass with fresh eyes, not a professional's sign-off. For anything consequential, a CPA, attorney, or fee-only planner still gets the last word.

        Why not copy every file into FI Review? You'd have two sets of files to keep current (a stale copy causes false alarms and misses), more copies of your documents in more places, and a reviewer that tends to redo the work instead of testing it.

        Want proof it's really checking? Run a drill once. Copy a real packet, change one thing before you paste it (a contribution limit it cites, or one calculated total), and send it to a new FI Review chat. A genuine review should catch it; if it doesn't, don't lean on it for that kind of check. Then delete that chat.

        After you retire: turning savings into a paycheck

        Retirement flips the job of your accounts. For decades they collected money; now they pay you. Most retirees rebuild a monthly paycheck from a few sources, drawn in a deliberate order so taxes stay low and the money lasts.

        Where the monthly paycheck comes from

        SourceHow it pays youHow it's taxed (federal)
        Social SecurityA monthly check for life, raised for inflation each year. You choose when it starts, between 62 and 70.Up to 85% can be taxable, depending on your other income. Many states, including Texas, don't tax it.
        PensionA monthly check, often with a survivor option you choose once, when you retire.Taxed as ordinary income.
        Taxable brokerageSell shares or take dividends as needed.Each year you owe tax on interest, dividends, and any gains a fund pays out, and on the gain when you sell. Qualified dividends and long-term gains get lower rates; some retirees owe 0% on them.
        Traditional 401(k), 403(b), 457(b), IRAWithdraw monthly or yearly. Required withdrawals start at 73, or 75 if you were born in 1960 or later.Withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income. Before 59½ there's a 10% penalty unless an exception fits: for example, a government 457(b) (its own money, not money rolled in from an IRA or another plan), your employer's plan if you left that job in or after the year you turned 55 (not IRAs), or a series of equal yearly payments.
        Roth IRA and Roth 401(k)Withdraw what you need once you're 59½ and the account is five years old; Roth IRA contributions can come out anytime.Tax-free, with no required withdrawals during your lifetime.
        HSAReimburse yourself for medical costs, including old receipts.Tax-free for medical costs. After 65, other withdrawals are taxed like a traditional IRA, with no penalty.
        Cash, CDs, Treasury billsYour spending buffer.Interest is taxed as income; Treasury interest isn't taxed by states.

        A common order to draw from

        1. Keep one to two years of spending in cash or short-term Treasuries, so a market drop never forces you to sell low.
        2. In the early years, before Social Security and required withdrawals, draw from taxable accounts, and use the low tax brackets for traditional withdrawals or Roth conversions.
        3. Start Social Security at the age your plan picked, often later for the higher earner, which also raises the survivor's check.
        4. Usually, let Roth money grow the longest. It's tax-free, has no required withdrawals, and is the best money to leave to heirs. Your own order depends on taxes, ACA premium help, and Medicare premiums; your AI models it.
        5. Use the HSA for medical costs along the way.

        The goal isn't the lowest tax this year; it's the lowest tax over your whole retirement. Your household project runs these numbers for you in the retirement income session.

        Setting up the monthly paycheck

        Choose one account as the paycheck source for the year, usually the taxable account or an IRA. Set an automatic transfer to checking on the same day each month. Have federal tax withheld from IRA withdrawals and from Social Security (Social Security lets you choose 7%, 10%, 12%, or 22%), or pay quarterly estimates. Refill the cash buffer once or twice a year, ideally when markets are up, and let the October annual review reset the plan.

        Taxes that change when you retire

        Payroll taxes stopSocial Security and Medicare taxes, 7.65% of wages for most employees, apply to paychecks, not to retirement withdrawals or benefits.
        Income tax depends on where the money comes fromTraditional withdrawals, pensions, and part of Social Security are taxed as income; qualified Roth withdrawals aren't; taxable accounts are taxed on interest, dividends, and gains. That's why the order matters.
        Bigger deductions at 65You get a higher standard deduction at 65, and from 2025 through 2028 an extra $6,000 senior deduction per person 65 or older, phasing out above $75,000 of income ($150,000 for couples).
        Health premiums follow your incomeRetire before 65 and marketplace (ACA) premium help depends on the income your withdrawals and Roth conversions create, so plan them together. After 65, a big withdrawal in one year can raise your Medicare premiums (IRMAA) two years later.
        State income tax variesSome states tax retirement income and some don't. Texas has no state income tax.
        Property tax breaks come at 65, not at retirementRetiring doesn't lower property tax, but turning 65 usually does. In Texas, school taxes are often the biggest part of the bill. On your primary home, school districts exempt $140,000 of its value for every homeowner and another $60,000 once you're 65, and your school tax on that home can't rise above the amount for the year you qualify (the tax ceiling), except for improvements. Homeowners 65 or older can also defer property taxes, with interest. Don't assume any of it happens automatically: apply with your county appraisal district. Other states have their own senior exemptions or freezes.
        Filing status can changeAfter one spouse dies, the survivor soon files as single, with narrower brackets. It's one reason to use low-tax years for Roth conversions.
